SpaceX’s Starship spacecraft was spotted in space using a new-generation Starlink satellite

Oleksandr Burlaka

We have just received an incredible new view of  SpaceX’s Starship, captured by one of the next-generation Starlink satellites. A video combining images from four satellite cameras shows the final stage of the flight and details of the spacecraft’s engine operations.

SpaceTechnology

Incredible Images of the World’s Most Powerful Rocket

Starship, the largest and most powerful rocket ever built, launched on its 13th test flight from South Texas last Friday, July 24. The suborbital journey was similar in many respects to previous Starship test missions, with one major exception: this time, the spacecraft deployed a group of operational next-generation Starlink internet satellites into  space, www.space.com reports.

Six of these Starlink V3 spacecraft were equipped with cameras so that SpaceX could obtain detailed images of the heat shield on Starship’s upper stage, also known simply as Ship. The satellites accomplished this task and more, capturing remarkable views of Ship as it moved away from them high above Earth.

SpaceX shared one such view on the social media platform X on Friday evening, July 31. The 65-second video combines images captured by four different cameras installed on a single Starlink V3 satellite.

The video begins with a close-up of the gleaming, 171-foot-tall, or 52-meter, spacecraft. More of the vehicle becomes visible as the camera angles change and the two spacecraft move farther apart. By the end of the video, Ship can be seen in full, along with signs of its activity: three of its six Raptor engines are operating, while small plumes of white vapor indicate that the spacecraft is using its thrusters for precise control.

The Starlink V3 Satellite Constellation

Like every previous Starship test mission, Flight 13 followed a suborbital trajectory. As a result, the 20 Starlink V3 satellites—the first of their kind to reach space—did not remain there for long. They returned to Earth approximately 20 minutes after deployment.

Their successors will travel higher, and there will be many of them. According to documents filed with the U.S. Federal Communications Commission, SpaceX plans to operate a megaconstellation consisting of 100,000 Starlink V3 satellites.

Starship will play a key role in building and maintaining this constellation, which will require numerous launches of the powerful, fully reusable rocket. SpaceX currently operates a constellation of nearly 11,000 Starlink satellites, but these V2 spacecraft are considerably smaller than the 2,000-kilogram V3 version.

SpaceTechnology

An Overall Successful Mission

Ship appears to have completed all of its objectives during Flight 13. After deploying the satellites and successfully restarting one of its Raptor engines in space, it made a targeted splashdown in the Indian Ocean off the coast of Western Australia approximately 65 minutes after launch.

The spacecraft survived its hazardous reentry through Earth’s atmosphere intact and, as far as is known, remains intact today. SpaceX recently shared a photograph showing the vehicle floating in the Indian Ocean five days after its landing.

However, Starship’s giant first-stage booster, known as Super Heavy, encountered several problems during Flight 13. For example, its landing sequence used only five of the planned 13 Raptor engines, and the booster struck the waters of the Gulf of Mexico at a higher speed than intended. Super Heavy has a total of 33 Raptor engines, all of which operate during liftoff.

Starship remains under development, but SpaceX intends to bring the megarocket into operation as quickly as possible. It is scheduled to fly as part of NASA’s crewed Artemis III mission to low Earth orbit in mid-2027 and to land a crew near the Moon’s south pole during Artemis IV in late 2028.

Artemis mission spacesuits are sent to be tested in a pool

Oleksandr Burlaka

The U.S.  space agency has tested the spacesuits that will be used during lunar missions under the Artemis program. Astronauts worked in them at the bottom of a giant pool at the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory.

SpaceTechnology

Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory

The American Artemis lunar program is gradually approaching its culmination—the moment when a human once again sets foot on the surface of our natural satellite. To accomplish this, astronauts will need specialized spacesuits. As www.space.com reports, preparations are proceeding at full speed.

The spacesuit’s principal developers are Axiom Space and the fashion house Prada. A recent test was conducted at the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory in Houston, Texas. Its central feature is a giant pool measuring 62 meters long, 41 meters wide, and 18 meters deep.

The reason NASA operates such a facility is simple: equipment for space missions must somehow be tested on Earth under conditions that resemble those of space as closely as possible. However, creating a large chamber combining a vacuum and weightlessness on Earth is practically impossible. A pool of this kind is therefore used as the closest available approximation.

Test Results

The latest test was also unusual because it sought to recreate not only conditions in space, but specifically the shadowed areas of the lunar surface. The bottom of the pool was covered with material simulating lunar regolith, while the surrounding environment was kept in complete darkness.

Astronauts are expected to land near the Moon’s south pole, where such conditions are entirely natural. They must therefore practice working under powerful spotlights, as demonstrated during the test at the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory.

The astronauts also practiced installing equipment on the simulated surface in the same manner in which they will perform these tasks on the Moon.

Astronomers finally measured the full size of the largest known galaxy

Oleksandr Burlaka

Researchers have obtained the deepest images of IC 1101 ever taken, allowing them to determine the galaxy’s outer boundary for the first time. The observations confirmed it as the largest known galaxy, extending approximately 520 kiloparsecs, or about 1.7 million light-years, and containing roughly 3.4 trillion solar masses in stars.

Galaxies That Are Difficult to Define

The largest galaxies, known as brightest cluster galaxies (BCGs), are the result of hierarchical galaxy formation. They grow by absorbing smaller galaxies over billions of years. Their faint stellar halos gradually merge into the intracluster medium—the diffuse gas and stars filling the space between galaxies within a cluster, phys.org reports.

This blending is precisely what makes BCGs so difficult to measure. Their outskirts contain evidence of past mergers in the form of tidal tails and stellar halos that overlap with the surrounding intracluster light, making it difficult to determine where the galaxy ends and the rest of the cluster begins.

A team led by Carlos Marrero-de-la-Rosa of the Instituto de Astrofísica de Canarias selected one of the most extended stellar systems ever observed: the BCG at the center of the Abell 2029 galaxy cluster. Its stellar extent had previously been measured at 607 kiloparsecs. Using ultra-deep images from the Wide Field Camera on the Isaac Newton Telescope (INT), the astronomers set out to identify the edge of this galaxy.

Determining the True Size of IC 1101

Measuring a galaxy’s true boundary requires detecting extremely faint starlight and separating it from an unwanted source of contamination: scattered light from foreground stars, which can spread a weak glow across an image and obscure the very faint regions astronomers are trying to detect.

The team created a detailed model of the telescope’s point spread function using dozens of calibration stars of different brightness levels. They then used this model to subtract scattered light from more than 250 stars in the final image, revealing faint structures.

Radial profiles—showing how starlight changes with distance from the center—place the edge at a radius of 260 kiloparsecs. This gives the galaxy a confirmed diameter of approximately 520 kiloparsecs, making it the largest galaxy known to date.

The measured edge radius of IC 1101 remains among the largest ever recorded for an individual galaxy, corresponding to a projected diameter of about 520 kiloparsecs. Its total stellar mass is estimated at approximately 3.4 trillion solar masses, depending on exactly where the boundary is drawn.

For comparison, the Milky Way extends only about 30 kiloparsecs. IC 1101 is approximately 17 times wider. Its stellar mass alone also exceeds the Milky Way’s entire mass, including dark matter, which is estimated at between 1 and 1.5 trillion solar masses.

Still Growing

Beyond the galaxy’s main boundary, the team detected eight large, faint, asymmetrical stellar structures. Two of them coincide with known X-ray “disturbances” in the surrounding hot cluster gas, providing evidence that the galaxy is still growing through continued accretion rather than existing as a completed, stable system.

The team writes that the outskirts of IC 1101 “clearly show signs of ongoing mass assembly,” indicating that the galaxy is still increasing in size.

This is consistent with an earlier X-ray study that identified similar disturbances as a likely sign of a past collision with another galaxy group approximately 2–3 billion years ago.
