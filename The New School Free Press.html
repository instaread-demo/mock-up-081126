Nearly 90 faculty and staff laid off, as efforts to slash employees continue

By:

Ronan Ocomartun and Shane Gomez

Sponsored by
0:00 / 5:57
Instaread

Nearly 90 faculty and staff members at The New School have been laid off over the past week. It’s the latest effort by university administrators to slash employees and cut costs amid a years-long operating deficit that’s now $60 million.

Many of those laid off are tenured, and several are the most prominent critics of the university’s restructuring. Others work in programs that were neither closed nor marked for redesign, and some work in programs “central” to the restructuring, according to a June 1 statement by The New School’s chapter of the American Association of University Professors (AAUP TNS). Additionally, “many” are “tenured faculty of color,” according to a BlueSky post by AAUP TNS.

Overall, at least 19 full-time faculty members and 68 staff members have been laid off, according to a university document and information from the Lang Faculty Council. Both were obtained and reviewed by the New School Free Press.

The 19 laid-off faculty all teach at Eugene Lang College of Liberal Arts and the New School for Social Research, according to the AAUP TNS Worker Solidarity Committee. Several laid-off faculty are those who were rehomed at those colleges after the Schools of Public Engagement was dissolved in 2025. 

Of the 68 laid off staff members, 16 belong to Local 1205, the union representing library and clerical staff at the university. Nine members of the 1205-Professional unit and seven members of the 1205-Clerical/Library unit will have their positions eliminated, Nelson Nuñez, president of Teamsters Local 1205, said. 

The other 52 staff positions have not immediately been identified by NSFP.

Laid-off employees were notified by HR on Monday, although some departments have held private meetings over the week to inform impacted faculty and staff beforehand. 

The most recent round of layoffs work as a six months’ notice; employees can continue working until they are terminated in December. Alternatively, they can choose, if offered, to resign immediately. 

NSFP has reached out to university communications and is awaiting a response.

The community reacts, questions university’s motives

Sanjay Reddy, professor of economics at NSSR and vocal critic of the university’s restructuring plan, was one of the laid off faculty.

A statement by the New School Economics Student Union on June 1 slammed the decision and questioned the university’s motives, calling him one of the “most prominent faculty voices challenging the administration’s account of the University’s finances.”

“Whether one agreed with his conclusions or not, criticism of institutional leadership lies at the heart of the university’s mission,” the Economics Student Union statement read. 

The Economics Student Union statement also highlighted that several other faculty “who have been actively participating in ongoing efforts to organize the faculty and who have raised alarm at the erosion of faculty governance norms” have also been laid off.

The AAUP TNS statement similarly condemned “in the strongest possible terms” the layoffs, many of which, they wrote, appear “politically motivated and retaliatory.”

“We deplore the lack of transparency and utter disregard of faculty governance structures in the entire restructuring process,” the AAUP TNS statement reads. 

Nuñez called the layoffs a “significant blow” to the entire TNS community.

“These are dedicated professionals who have devoted years — many of them decades — of service to advancing the academic mission of this institution,” Nuñez said. “Their contributions have helped support students, faculty, and the day-to-day operations that make the university function.”

Many of the employees received notification of the elimination of their positions through an unsigned email, Nuñez added. He said the Local 1205 will continue to challenge the decisions and advocate for staff.

Layoffs are part of a sprawling, larger effort to cut employees

The layoff figures so far are less than anticipated. Previously, 15% of faculty and staff were expected to be laid off. The AAUP TNS Worker Solidarity Committee said that the lower-than-expected numbers were due, in part, to the community’s organizing last academic year, which included four demonstrations.

The figures are also lower, according to the AAUP TNS Worker Solidarity Committee, due to other quiet means of forcing out employees, the threat of non-renewed contracts, incentivizing step-downs, or leaving out of fear of layoffs. 

And there have been other means of cutting employees. “Eligible” faculty, staff, and administrators have received a myriad of buyout offers, known as “Voluntary Separation” offers, and new retirement options, like the “Voluntary Early Retirement Plan” and phased retirement plans. 

These “voluntary separation programs” have resulted in a 7% cut to faculty and staff, according to a March email from administrators.

The whole project aims to cut the university’s total number of employees by 20%. There will be 65 fewer full-time faculty next fall, as a result, the Chronicle of Higher Education reported on Tuesday. Other cost-cutting efforts to stabilize the operating deficit have included eliminating majors, minors, classes, and services.

Many community members strongly disagree with the austerity measures. 

Laying off faculty and staff would only reduce costs in the short term while failing to address the real source of TNS’s financial troubles, the Economic Students Union statement said, citing declining enrollment — which is expected to drop by 500 students from an already historic low — and poor student retention.

“The University’s difficulties were not created by tenured faculty, academic programs, learning center staff, or administrative assistants,” the Economic Students Union statement read. “They are the result of years of financial mismanagement, unsustainable debt burdens, declining enrollment, and strategic choices made at the highest levels of the institution.”

AAUP TNS’s statement connected the university’s decisions to broader, nationwide trends of corporatizing higher education and called the cuts “a great irony” that conflict with the university’s mission and history.

“[TNS] is at the forefront of the alarming trend in U.S. higher education towards gutting academic freedom and shared governance, destroying the protections of tenure, and hollowing out intellectual and creative practice in favor of the dictates of the bottom line,” the AAUP TNS statement read.
